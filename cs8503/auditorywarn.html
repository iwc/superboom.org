Summary: Learning and Retention of Auditory Warnings

Authors: Ying KLeung, Sean Smith, Simon Parker, Russell Martin

Overview

This paper discusses a study that evaluated the ability of users to learn and retain mappings of sounds to corresponding events. The experiment compared abstract sounds (beeps, alarms), speech warnings and auditory icons (environmental sounds). Several sessions were held with eighteen subjects over several days. The first of three trial days consisted of a learning and a test phase for each sound category. The following two sessions consisted only of a test phase, intended to test the longer term retention of sound/event mappings. Users were asked to identify a series of events, based solely on the auditory cue. The learning of sound/event mappings was measured by the number of trials needed during the test phase for each user to complete two test trials with 100% accuracy. Retention was measured by recording the number of errors in identification that occurred in the trials of the latter two days (each of which lacked a training phase).

Interesting Results

  • Both speech cues and auditory icons exhibited statistically significant improvements in both learning and retention when compared to abstract sounds.
  • On the first day, the learning phase required eight trials for completion, versus 2-3 trials for speech and auditory icons.
  • On days two and three, speech and auditory icons were misidentified under two times on average, while abstract sounds were misidentified an average of about four times.
  • Speech cues and auditory icons were closely matched in performance for learning and retention. The authors suggest that auditory icons have the additional advantage of not interfering with other speech in the environment, and they are more recognizable in noisy environments.
