Summary: Capturing, Structuring, and Representing Ubiquitous Audio

Authors: Debby Hindrus, Chris Schmandt, Chris Horner

Overview

This paper explores ways to capture audio information that allows for convenient and useful access at to that data at a later time. The authors focus on the storage of phone conversations to be used for later reference. Phone calls can be recorded directly through ISDN facilities, and the sound is processed to extract structural data for graphical display. Speaker pauses are detected simply by noise levels, and speaker changes (where one person in the conversation stops speaking, and the other member starts speaking) are detected with a redundant microphone on the local side (where the application is running). This information is then displayed with a set of tools designed to provide meaningful graphic representations, and easy access to raw audio data. The basic audio display tool provides the ability to rapidly preview sound clips by selectively skipping short segments of the sound clip for a faster overall playback. The user can use this to review clips to determine their informational value. The user also has the option of saving or discarding sound clips as they are captured during the conversation. Those clips deems worthy of storage can then be used as an embeddable data type in other applications, such as a calendar application or as an email attachment.

Interesting Ideas/Questions

  • This application set is a wonderful example of using contextual information to aid in structuring of data. Since phone conversations tend to follow a back and forth shift from one speaker to the other (with pauses in between) it is fairly easy to determine speaker changes and mark boundaries of speech segments.
  • The user has a high degree of flexibility to determine what data is useful, and the rest can be discarded. The limitation of stored data can greatly simplify later access.
  • The basic tools provide the user with a fast means of searching and reviewing large masses of audio data. This capability seems to be an important ingredient for natural, simple access.
  • This paper seems to focus on a ubiquitous method of capture, but the later access is limited to workstation-centric applications. What about a ubiquitous access method?
