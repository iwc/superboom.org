Summary: Designing Auditory Interactions for PDAs

(Panel Proceedings)

Panelists: Debby Hindus, Barry Arons, Lisa Stifelman, Bill Gaver, Elizabeth Mynatt, Maribeth Back
 

Overview

This paper details comments made at a UIST'95 panel discussing the technical and social aspects of audio-centric interaction techniques for Personal Digital Assistants. This summary simply sums up those points in an extremely concise format.

Panelist: Lisa J. Stifelman

  • Presently, few PDA-class devices with any form of useful auditory input exist, and most have only limited audio output.
  • Designers should not try to solve auditory interaction problems with visual techniques, nor should they simply translate a visual interface directly into an auditory interface.
  • Interaction in a PDA context implies unbounded, ongoing interaction sessions.
  • The physical aspect of such a device can have a significant impact on the style of auditory interaction (a wearable device versus a handheld device, versus a wristwatch-like device).
  • With a ubiquitous device, social contexts become much more significant factors in interaction styles.
  • The acoustic properties of some environments may be detrimental to the device's perception of sounds, while other environments may be helpful. The entire range of environments in which such a device might be used must be accounted for by designers.
  • Auditory interfaces are in great need of an interaction metaphor, analogous to the desktop metaphor of the Apple Macintosh.

Panelist: Elizabeth D. Mynatt

  • Effective interfaces must find a way to support the concept of direct manipulation in an auditory context, since PDAs lack the screen real estate of other computing devices.
  • Designers must determine the proper balance between speech and non speech audio cues for their application. Speech cues frequently take longer for a user to decode and recognize, although they are less ambiguous.
  • People rely on auditory feedback in everyday, non computing oriented tasks, and this reliance can be used in abstract computing metaphors.
  • Auditory cues may be used to codify information for the monitoring the state of physical environments.

Panelist: Barry Arons

  • An all audio interface may allow ubiquitous computing devices to be made still smaller, and be useful in many contexts where traditional interfaces are unusable (hands are occupied, the user must look elsewhere at all times, etc.).
  • Ubiquitous devices of this nature should not only support auditory input and output, but provide extensive hardware and software support for audio as the primary data type for storage and interpretation.
  • The balance of visual and auditory elements, and the level of support for audio as data is highly dependent on the application.

Panelist: Bill Gaver

  • The potential for annoyance from audio only interfaces in everyday contexts is high. Other people do not desire to listen to an interaction between a user and their PDA.
  • Users can easily become engrossed in the virtual experience of an interaction session with their PDA, which can be troublesome if the user is working with private personal data, or professional proprietary information.
  • These ubiquitous devices can act as an auditory conduit for new types of social interactions.
  • Careful design of these interfaces can avoid the social context problems, and also extend their capacity as a social conduit.

Panelist: Maribeth Back

  • Personalization of sound cues on PDAs could increase acceptance and comfort of the devices by users.
  • Sound cues, or auditory icons should appeal to the end user's "aural template" of that sound.
  • The designer may use a "narrative context" to shift the user's mind to an appopriate interaction context or abuse such a shift(Ex: change from mechanistic sounds to animal sounds).
  • The use of narrative in sound clips can provide more information than just the presense or absence of the sound. A sound can easily tell a "minature story" of moving from one state to another.
  • A careful analysis of such a narrative can allow the sound designer to abbrieviate the sound to its critical elements, for a more rapid response and recognition time for the user.
  • High quality audio reproduction is necessary to maintain the designer's intent, poor reproduction may undo a designer's careful work.
